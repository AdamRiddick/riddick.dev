- Make the writing useful and selective rather than filling a publishing schedule.
- Give team effort proper weight; show where Adam fitted in and what could continue without him.

- Avoid arrogant or vain accounts of professional achievements.
- Use the same coherent visual identity across articles and other site material.

## Working prose choices

These are editorial defaults to test against Adam's revisions, not statistically observed traits of a complete writing corpus.

**Direct, with some warmth.** Sound like an experienced engineer discussing a real problem with a capable colleague. Make the point plainly and leave room for a different context. Contractions are welcome when natural.

**Measured confidence.** State a position and explain why. Name the constraint that limits it rather than repeatedly adding "perhaps", "probably" or "it depends". Adam's exploratory chat messages should not make finished articles perpetually hesitant.

**Connected reasoning.** Let a paragraph develop an idea. Mix sentence lengths as the explanation needs. Use a short sentence for emphasis occasionally; avoid a sequence of slogans or a dramatic line break after every sentence.

**Concrete language.** Prefer specific actions, boundaries, responsibilities and consequences. Introduce technical terms where they make the reasoning more precise and explain them when the audience needs it. Avoid a glossary lecture or an unexplained collection of acronyms.

**A team perspective.** Use "I" for Adam's contribution, responsibility or judgement and "we" only where a real team and shared work are supported. Recognise the engineers, product colleagues and operators involved. Do not replace every "I" with "we" and lose accountability.

**Respect for context.** Explain what earlier teams were working with. A legacy system is evidence of accumulated requirements and decisions; it is not a convenient villain. Include a real compromise or a reason the alternative might be appropriate elsewhere.

**Ownership without heroics.** Show what makes the system understandable, operable and changeable by the team. The article need not end in personal triumph. Where the topic warrants it, discuss what remained unfinished or what would justify revisiting the decision.

**British English.** Use organisation, modernisation, behaviour, prioritise and judgement. Preserve exact product names, quoted source text and code spelling. Correct conversational typos rather than adopting them as a mannerism.

## Openings, structure and endings

Start with a recognisable situation or a specific question worth answering. Establish the practical stakes quickly. Avoid generic openings about a rapidly changing technology landscape, unearned first-person anecdotes and a provocative claim that the article does not substantiate.

The reader should understand the central decision early. Context, options, trade-offs, consequences and limits are useful reasoning prompts. Use natural section titles for the actual argument; do not impose the same six headings on every article.

Lists help when the reader has genuine choices, steps or checks to compare. A table should make a trade-off easier to assess. Neither is a substitute for explaining the relationship between the points.

End with a practical implication, a question worth taking to the team or the conditions under which the recommendation changes. Avoid restating the entire article, adding an inspirational maxim or turning the conclusion into a sales pitch.

## Habits to avoid

- Generic AI filler, inflated adjectives and unsupported claims of novelty.
- Boilerplate phrases such as "in today's fast-paced landscape", "delve into" or "game-changing".
- Repeated "X isn't about Y; it's about Z" constructions and artificial rhetorical questions.
- Universal instructions dressed up as personal authority: always, never, the only correct way.
- False precision, invented numbers, invented dialogue or retrospective certainty about uncertain decisions.
- Repeated branding phrases, decorative military/engineering metaphors or attempts to imitate another writer's catchphrases.
- Sentiment, humour, exclamation marks or profanity added simply to sound human. Warmth should arise from how people and their work are treated.

These are working editing choices. They do not override a specific stylistic request from Adam or accurate quoted material.

## Constructed calibration example

The following is an agent-written example of the proposed direction. It is not a quote, evidence of a particular project or an approved voice sample:

> A system can be running well and still be difficult to own. If the team needs to find its original architect whenever a decision comes up, there is still work to do.
>
> For me, seeing something through includes making sure the next person can understand why it was built that way, what the trade-offs were and where they can safely change it. That takes shared decisions, useful documentation and time with the people who will be responsible for it.

Use future edits to test this direction. Do not repeatedly reproduce this wording in articles.
