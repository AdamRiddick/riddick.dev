On one project I worked on, the team spent several months building before showing the client what had been delivered. The client was frustrated. The work missed their expectations, and it didn't deliver the outcome they needed. There had been something we could have shown a few weeks in. By the time we found out we were heading in the wrong direction, the team had spent much of the intervening period building on it.

The early version still needed work. Its value at that point was that the client could have looked at something concrete and told us whether it was taking them towards the outcome they needed. That was a useful decision to make while the team still had options.

## What the team could have learned sooner

I would have taken the first piece we could show and agreed with the client what it was meant to demonstrate. Give someone who knows the work a representative task and ask them to walk through it. Where would they get stuck? Could they use what came out the other end? What would they still need to do outside the system? A client can agree with a description of a feature and still discover, on trying it, that it solves the wrong part of their problem.

The review only helps if the answer can change the plan. If the client points out that the result is unusable, the team needs a chance to stop building on that assumption and decide what to do next. Some of the work may carry over. Some of it may need to go. Both are easier decisions while fewer things depend on it.

There are good reasons to hold back a production release. A change might need security checks, operational preparation or a coordinated rollout. A walkthrough in a test environment can still give the client enough to judge the outcome. The key is to be clear about what the demonstration can answer. A mock-up might be enough to check whether a workflow makes sense. If the concern is whether the output can support a real decision, the client needs a representative example of that output.

## What the wait can cost

I would start with the time between the first useful review and the point the client actually saw the work. That is the period in which effort could have gone elsewhere if the review had changed the plan. To show the scale, take a rounded six-month window and an illustrative budget. These figures are assumptions for the example. The project's actual costs and team size are private.

| Cost in the example | Assumption | Six months |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Delivery team, including employment or contract costs | £45,000 per month | £270,000 |
| Project support outside the team, such as governance and coordination | £5,000 per month | £30,000 |
| **Total cost exposed to the late decision** | **£50,000 per month** | **£300,000** |

The £300,000 is the cost of keeping the work going through that window. How much earlier feedback could have saved depends on what the team would have changed. If half the spending in this example went towards work an early review would have prevented, that would be about £150,000 of effort the organisation could have redirected before allowing for the cost of the review and any earlier correction. If the team stayed employed or under contract, the benefit would mainly be capacity for other work.

Then there is the work after the mismatch becomes clear. Someone has to establish what the client needs, work out what can be reused and agree a revised plan. The client may need to spend more time reviewing a second attempt while continuing to work without the outcome they expected. Other commitments may have to move to make room. I would record that recovery effort separately and count people and costs only once. The value of the delayed outcome is a separate question again. It needs evidence about what the client would have been able to do sooner.

## Put the review where it can change a decision

Before committing a team to the next stretch of work, I would agree with the client what we can show, who can judge it and what we will do if it misses the mark. Start with the assumption that would be most expensive to get wrong. If it takes months to make the full service usable, find a smaller piece of evidence that lets the client judge that assumption sooner.

On this project, that opportunity came a few weeks in. The next commitment should have depended on what we learned from it.
